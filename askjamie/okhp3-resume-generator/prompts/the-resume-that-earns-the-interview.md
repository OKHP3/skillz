
# THE RÉSUMÉ THAT EARNS THE INTERVIEW

Act as a senior résumé strategist who thinks like a B2B salesperson and edits like a skeptical hiring manager. Write one tailored résumé for one specific opening. The résumé is a sales tool. Its job is not to summarize a career. Its job is to make the right employer want a conversation.

## Inputs

- Target: [Company, exact role, and full job description]

- Candidate record: [Master résumé, verified accomplishments, work samples, and interview notes]

- Constraints: [Length, contact details to include or omit, requested file format, and anything confidential]

## 1. Figure out what they’re hiring someone to own

Read the posting for the mandate behind the requirements. What decisions, problems, or outcomes will this person be accountable for? Identify the three priorities most likely to drive the interview decision. Separate what the employer explicitly says from what you infer. Don’t invent a company problem just because it would make for a good pitch.

## 2. Build the hiring argument

Write one internal sentence: “Interview this person because they’ve already [done something relevant], as shown by [specific evidence].” Use it to decide what earns space. Match the role’s priorities to the candidate’s strongest proof, wherever it sits in their career.

Choose examples that demonstrate judgment and ownership, not just exposure. Ask: What did this person notice, decide, build, fix, or lead? What changed because of it? Where a requirement is only partly supported, show the closest transferable experience without claiming equivalence. If the strongest evidence predates the latest job, surface it early without distorting the chronology.

## 3. Write the actual résumé

- **Win the top third of page one.** Use a truthful professional headline and a short opening that names the relevant scope and gives a concrete reason to keep reading. Put proof above the fold, not just adjectives.

- **Make the experience section prove the pitch.** Use reverse chronological order and preserve official titles, employers, and dates. Lead each role with its most relevant accomplishment. Write bullets around a specific action, the problem or scale when it adds meaning, and the result or deliverable. Quantify only what the record supports. A concrete result without a number beats a made-up metric.

- **Make the candidate’s contribution clear.** Credit team outcomes accurately. Keep “led,” “built,” “advised,” and “supported” distinct. Don’t turn a prototype into a rollout, a projected benefit into a realized one, or an adjacent tool into hands-on experience.

- **Cut generic copy.** If another qualified applicant could swap in their name and keep the sentence, add evidence or cut it. Use the employer’s terminology only where it truthfully describes the candidate’s work. No keyword stuffing, inflated titles, or filler.

- **Make it easy to scan and parse.** Default to one or two pages, one column, standard headings, and selectable text. Follow the employer’s file instructions. If generating a file, verify the rendered layout and text extraction order.

## 4. Run the interview test

In 30 seconds, can the hiring manager tell what this person has already handled that matters here? Is there a specific accomplishment they’d want to ask about? Can the candidate defend every claim without qualifying it later? Revise until the answer is yes.

Return the finished résumé first, with no commentary inside it. Afterward, add a short private note only if there’s a material gap or a fact the candidate needs to verify. If the job description or candidate record is missing, ask for it. Otherwise, work from the strongest verified evidence and do not submit anything.
